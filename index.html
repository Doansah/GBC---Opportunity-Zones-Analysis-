Background

What Are Opportunity Zones?

Opportunity Zones are federally designated census tracts where investors can receive significant capital gains tax benefits in exchange for long-term investment. Created by the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, the original program designated nearly 9,000 tracts nationwide through 2026.

The 2027 re-designation gives governors a second chance to choose which distressed communities receive this powerful incentive. Maryland can designate up to 25% of its 451 eligible tracts — roughly 113 communities — as Opportunity Zones. Unlike the 2018 round, this cycle benefits from years of research on what actually made Opportunity Zones work, and what didn't.

The Greater Baltimore Committee partnered with researchers to apply that evidence to Maryland's specific landscape. Our goal: identify the Goldilocks tracts — communities that genuinely need investment and have enough economic infrastructure to actually attract private capital. Not too distressed that investors stay away. Not already-thriving areas where the tax break is redundant. Just right.

Our Approach

How We Evaluated Tracts

We evaluated Maryland's 451 eligible tracts through a three-stage process grounded in research from the first OZ round, particularly Ohio's analysis of what separated successful designations from underperforming ones.

  • Hard Filters Eliminate clearly unviable tracts (fewer than 200 jobs AND poverty above 45% AND zero building permits — designation would go unused) and already-thriving tracts where income exceeds the metro median and home values are in the top quartile (capital flows regardless of OZ status).
  • Weighted Scoring Surviving tracts receive a composite score across three dimensions: Investment Viability, Community Need, and Incentive Stackability. The scoring weights reflect what Ohio research found to be the strongest predictors of OZ investment activity.
  • Recommendation The top-ranked tracts — up to 113 — are marked as recommended designations. Individual tract profiles will be developed for the top 20–30 candidates to inform final decisions.
The Goldilocks Zone Research from the first OZ round shows the strongest results come from tracts with moderate distress (20–35% poverty rate) combined with an existing economic base (job density, commercial activity, permit activity). Extreme distress with no economic infrastructure repels private capital; low distress with high home values means the incentive isn't needed. We're looking for the middle ground.
